Somewhere Else

By Jim Crane

Somewhere in the long and distinguished history of human folly, there is a corner reserved for people who pay thousands for a JPEG (everyone's word for it, even when the file is a PNG, a gif, a film, a file or even, god forbid, a song) and later discover that the JPEG was never theirs, never in their wallet, and never anywhere near a blockchain. It was on a server, in the care of a company, and the bill has since gone unpaid. The NFT you own is not the JPEG. It is a receipt for the JPEG, with a small note attached that says “ask them”.

I should declare an interest, in the sense that someone who has spent years building a sandcastle is interested in the tide. I have spent several years building on Stacks, and some of it is, as I write, quietly turning into very expensive 404s, the internet's polite way of saying “page not found”. A few months ago I set out to find out whether that was just my collections or everybody's, and I wanted evidence, not anecdotes. So I took the 250 biggest collections on Stacks by all-time volume and checked each one twice: once through its own contract, and once the way the rest of us actually look at NFTs, on a marketplace. Of the 241 that were art, 34 (14%) can no longer reach their own artwork through their own contract. Sixteen are still on show only because a marketplace kept a copy: a photograph whose negative was lost years ago, the whole archive one print on a stranger's fridge, one over-enthusiastic wipe-down from oblivion.

The blockchain, in other words, is a famously honest accountant and a famously useless archivist. It remembers every transfer to the second. It just has no idea where the JPEGs went.

How did I get here?

For about twenty years I was a music producer who ran a recording studio, and for much of that a sound engineer and tour manager, which is the job where you discover exactly how many people can be owed money at one in the morning. I found Bitcoin on a tour bus in 2011, and it ended, as these things do, with me remortgaging my house at the end of 2013 to import the UK's first Bitcoin ATM from Canada. As financial decisions go, it was, let's say, committed.

The machine drew a crowd of very clever people, one of whom introduced me to smart contracts, and by 2017 I was sketching the idea I actually cared about: contracts for the music business. When a band walks off stage having played its set, the stage manager ticks one final box, the contract's terms have been fulfilled, and everyone, band and crew, is paid in the same instant. Anyone who has ever chased a festival invoice for months will understand why I thought this was the future. I still do.

Perfect contracts, imaginary JPEGs

A few years later I was introduced to Blockstack, which became Stacks, and in 2021 I cofounded This Is Number One, the first curated NFT marketplace on Stacks. We wrote some of the first NFT contracts on Stacks, with early, rudimentary royalty payments built in, before SIP-009 gave NFTs a standard. For our Genesis collection we commissioned the artist Chemical X, who pulled together musicians including Dave Stewart from Eurythmics, Fatboy Slim and Orbital. Each artist chose a charity, and when a sale went through the contract was designed to split the money automatically. No invoices. No chasing. No one in a back office “looking into it”. Alongside them was Cara Delevingne, whose one-off NFT “Mine” was announced in May 2021 and sold on 10 July for 18,000 STX, about $21,000 at the time, the first commercial NFT sale on Stacks (my tweet, Cointelegraph, Gamma).

It was an exciting time, watching a new network being born and making our mark on it. I was a boy carving his initials into a young tree. But it also had a flaw that none of us noticed, because the flaw wasn't in the contract. The contract was immaculate about the financial side. About the JPEGs and the films it was a good deal more relaxed, a host who has invited two hundred guests and given no thought to where they'll sit. The art lived somewhere else, and “somewhere else” is the sort of phrase that ought to set off alarms in anyone who has ever asked a letting agent where their deposit is and been told it's “being held elsewhere”.

We did have a plan. The plan was Gaia, the storage layer that came with Stacks: files kept somewhere you control, with the chain keeping track of where. It folded storage back in on itself, Stacks files for Stacks tokens, instead of leaving everything to IPFS, and on paper it looked like the answer, which is where most things look their best. In practice our developer was ahead of the game: it was his opinion that Gaia wasn't getting the support it needed at the time, and in the end it lost all support. Being early, as I know better than most, often feels almost exactly like being wrong. The files were big high-definition audio and video, and the alternative suggestion at the time was Cloudinary, which is built for heavy media and is very fast. We accepted at once. Sometimes pioneering has a sound, and that sound is someone typing “LFG!!!” with the confidence of a person who has never once been rugged by a hosting company.

Imagine selling someone a painting and then renting them the wall, indefinitely, out of your own pocket, while the new owner admires the brushwork and never once wonders who is paying for the plaster. That is, near enough, what happened. Cloudinary is a subscription, and what nobody said out loud (or not loudly enough to be heard over the ringing in my ears, a souvenir of twenty years of soundchecks) was that somebody would have to keep paying it for as long as the art was meant to exist. For art, that is a long time. For digital art it is worse, because a file does not fade gracefully; it works perfectly right up until the day it simply stops turning up.

That somebody was Chemical X, the artist who pulled together our Genesis collection and then produced many of the earliest collections on the nascent network. For years he paid to keep work that people had already bought from going dark. Not to sell more of it, not to promote anything, just to keep the links alive. From 2021 to 2026 the cost ran into the thousands of pounds. Looking back, it seems obvious that we would end up here one day, staring at an empty frame someone paid real money for, wondering whether the art is ever coming back and whether anyone left a forwarding address.

Back in 2021, in answers drafted for a press interview, he explained why he was minting on Stacks: “Opensource, decentralised apps that give us all back control of our information and other assets can only be a good thing. ‘Can’t be evil’ is the way to go.” The contracts kept that promise. The hosting, bought in good faith, wasn’t built the same way.

Ordinals, or a short education in “forever”

Ordinals, when they arrived, taught me more about permanence than anything had before. The idea, if you haven't come across it, is that the Bitcoin blockchain is in effect one enormous shared hard drive, which will keep being mined for as long as anyone has an incentive to run it, a motive more reliable than any mission statement, and you can put your data straight into it. Not a link to your data. The data. That is permanence properly understood, and it was a revelation to someone who had just watched the other kind being billed monthly.

The catch was cost. Fees kept rising, and I got rather good at compression, in the way tour managers get good at packing for budget airlines: my first Audionals song, Truth, needed about 100 KB; I got that down to about 30 KB, then a twelve-minute song down to about 3 KB (CryptoNews); and eventually I managed to squeeze an 8.5-minute song into a sub-1 KB text file of instructions for building the audio and synth elements on chain (source). I had to admit, however, that it was not the answer for working musicians, a group not historically known for their spare capital, who need things to be faster and a great deal cheaper than layer one can manage.

Which sent me back to Stacks, where I'd started. I’d been building for Bitcoin the whole time, by a route with more turns than my first driving test, where a three-point turn took about seven and ended with me facing the way I started. First Stacks, then Ordinals, then Stacks again. I built Xtrata to support what I'd been attempting with Audionals on bitcoin, and discovered it could support rather more than that. Stacks is secured by Bitcoin, which settles a block about every ten minutes, but it has room for ten to twenty times more transactions in each of those windows. It also has smart contracts: small programs that live on the chain and run the same way for everyone. An Xtrata inscription writes a file into the chain itself, in small pieces, so those contracts can read it, check it and act on it. On Bitcoin, an inscription is a letter sealed in a time capsule: permanent, but nothing on the chain can open it and act on what it says. On Stacks, a contract can open the capsule, check that the letter hasn't been tampered with, and carry on accordingly. Permanent data that a program can actually use is the whole trick, and it's what Forever Twins is built on.

The day the JPEGs went

It started with a trip back to Gamma, the marketplace where most Stacks NFTs are traded, to look at the This Is Number One tokens. Many of them were blank. Not dramatically blank, not a scene in a courtroom; just a grey square where a JPEG used to be, politely suggesting I try again later.

The tokens were all there. Owners, prices, transfer history, all present and correct: a perfect guest register for a hotel that had quietly been demolished. It was the JPEGs that had gone. On 16 October, when I asked Cloudinary for one of our videos, it replied with a single line that I've been unable to stop thinking about:

That's the whole epitaph for years of invoices. The tokens are fine, by the way. They're in people's wallets and you can trade them. They just point at an account that doesn't exist any more.

What Chemical X makes of it. I sent him a draft of this piece. “You might want to cull a few commas,” he replied, “but in essence it's approved by me.” He added: “I do feel disabled as it happens…” He puts the hosting bill at about £100 a month, which, if it ran the whole five years, comes to around £6,000 (my arithmetic, not his).

The images we did end up trusting to Gaia fared no better: Hiro switched off its hosted Gaia hub in June 2025, so the This Is Number One images still pointing there don't load either. There was no right answer in 2021. There were only different ways to rent.

You can see it for yourself on Gamma. Scroll the V2 page and nearly every tile is a grey box, which is minimalism, but not by choice. The few that load are the Hash Ones, little pixel-block pieces we deliberately made tiny, and there are 91 of them, split two ways. The 31 uploaded in September 2021, including #1, #4 and #25, sit on the same Chemical X account as everything else, so they are blank like the rest. The other 60, uploaded in early October 2021, sit on a second Cloudinary account that is still switched on, which is why those are the ones you can see. Why that second account is still alive I can't tell you. It holds small files, and a free plan would explain it, but that is a guess.

The original Genesis collection is stranger still. Gamma shows all five pieces, which sounds reassuring until you notice that they're stills. The films aren't what you're looking at: Gamma's record for the piece I checked holds only the still. All five are set up the same way. Each one's metadata lists the film in two places: a file in the same IPFS folder as the still, and a copy on Cloudinary, on the account that no longer exists. Gamma shows you the still, and the copy the film was built around is the one that's gone. But the IPFS copy of Mine, the piece I mentioned earlier, still plays. So the work isn't lost. It's still there, but only because somebody is still keeping a file that nobody looks at. That was the moment the question changed. It stopped being “what happened to our files?” and became “how many other collections are standing on somebody else's subscription?”

So I counted

The 250 are ranked by all-time volume on Gamma, and each got two checks. First, the honest way: read the token URI straight off the contract, follow it to the metadata, follow that to the image, and try a few IPFS gateways if needed. Second, the shop-window view: load the images Gamma itself serves and take them on trust. I also checked who is still hosting the IPFS files, whether the owner can still change the URI, and whether anything at all lives on chain.

None of the services involved would talk to a normal script, so the entire survey ran as code pasted into a browser tab sitting on Gamma, kept awake by a little background worker because browsers throttle tabs you aren't looking at. Inelegant, but effective. About 40 collections needed checking by eye. Nine weren't art, which left 241.

Here's where they stood on 13 October:

So 34 collections, 14%, can't reach their own art through their own contract any more. That's about 2.5 million STX of all-time volume, 18% of everything in the survey, and roughly 177,000 tokens. Three of the top ten. Seven of the top 25. Exactly one collection in the whole 241, Stacks Invaders, can draw its own art from its own contract. One.

The flip side is the number that stuck with me. Only 27 collections, 11%, are healthy or on chain. Everything else is either broken already or resting on a single host: an entire house running off one extension lead, plugged into a neighbour's garage.

It's a sample, taken once, from one network, so on 16 October I re-ran all 34 broken collections, with Megapont Ape Club as a control. All 34 are still broken. The only real change was This Is Number One (V2), which dropped from one working image to none. Ours, naturally.

A field guide to how digital art dies

Not with a rug pull, mostly. With admin.

One JSON file, thirteen collections. A whole cluster from the Stacks Art days, about 1.19 million STX of volume and around 21,700 tokens between them, point every single token at one JSON file on stacksart.com. Go there now and you get the website instead, which is not what any of them were hoping for. Not one of the thirteen is healthy. Citadels, 2,222 tokens, shows nothing at all. Zombie Wabbits is hanging on by a single image, one file that happens to still be sitting on the old site.

Same team, two set-ups. Megapont Ape Club is the biggest Stacks collection by volume, by miles, and it's one of the healthiest: IPFS, five separate hosts. The Megapont Robots family, five contracts and about 136,000 tokens, pointed at Megapont's own API instead. That API now returns a "Service Suspended" page, and so does the separate server behind the Megapont Space Agency spacesuits, so the Gamma tiles are blank. The Robot Factory and Space Agency sites are still online, but I couldn't find any of the art on them. Same team, same era, opposite outcome, purely down to where the files went.

The Robots are a case of a founder carrying the cost for as long as it made sense. Mark McKenzie, Megapont's co-founder, said publicly in 2023 that the project had been running at a loss, with him as the sole funder, and that the aim was a business model that didn't lean on the community's money. A server is a monthly bill, and a monthly bill is the first thing to lapse on a project paying its way out of one person's pocket. These are casualties of a model that asks a founder to fund the art forever, not of a founder who stopped caring, and it's exactly the case Forever Twins is for: once the files are on chain, nobody has to keep the lights on, and the people who built the thing can step back without the collection going dark.

That pattern holds across the whole survey, and it isn't the one IPFS sceptics expect. Of the 16 collections on their own servers, 9 are broken (56%). Of the 203 on IPFS, 4 are (2%). IPFS isn't the problem. The problem is the server, which only stays up for as long as somebody keeps paying for it, and life has a way of getting in the way of that.

The gateway that closed. Nine collections hardcoded Cloudflare's IPFS gateway into their URIs. Cloudflare stopped serving IPFS on 14 August 2024. Six of the nine were fine, because someone else still had the files. Three weren't: two Boomboxes cycles now only exist as Gamma's cached copy, and Stacks Roots is gone. The gateway didn't lose anything. It just showed that nobody else was holding a copy.

It isn't only the 2021 stuff. Yes, 2021 is the worst year: 28% broken, against 8% for 2022 and nothing broken from 2023 or 2024. But two collections that launched in October 2025 are already lost, both on their own servers, both with the URI locked so nobody can point them anywhere else. A year. That's all they got.

The bit that keeps me up

The lost collections are sad, but at least they're honest. You open them and there's nothing there. What keeps me up is the sixteen I've called walking dead.

With those, the contract's URI is dead, but Gamma still shows the art. In fifteen of them every image I sampled loaded; in the last one, eight out of ten. They're about 1.5 million STX of volume and roughly 28,600 tokens, and they include the third and fourth biggest collections in the survey. If you browse them, nothing looks wrong at all. It's a restaurant with a spotlessly well-prepared dining room in front of an empty kitchen.

[image: A candle-lit dining room, immaculately laid, while through the swing doors the kitchen is a bare empty room with no stove, and a nervous waiter looks on]

Stacks Punks V3, number four, deserves a paragraph to itself, and gets one. Every one of its 9,523 tokens points at the same single file, and that file describes exactly one picture: one shared image, with no token numbers, no traits and no per-token art in it. The contract has, in effect, never known what any individual punk looks like. Gamma does: its copy of the art sits in a folder on its own Pinata account, which is why the punks still show up on its pages. And because the contract has no freeze, its owner could repoint that link at any time.

[image: Stacks Punks V3, one file for everyone: a grid of generic pixel portraits all pointing at one punks.json card showing a single picture, and a man scratching his head asking who is number 4,812]

And Gamma's copies aren't spread around either: 214 of the 241 collections have their marketplace images served out of one Pinata account. Blocks, 2,500 pieces, is the scariest near miss. Its contract points at a dead file, Gamma's index points most tokens at a dead server, and the full set of images is sitting in one IPFS folder that only one pinning account holds. It's not gone. It's one bad Tuesday away from gone.

The good news is that 25 of the 34 broken collections can still have their URIs changed by whoever holds the keys. This stuff is fixable. It's just that nobody's looking.

A friendly look over the fence

[image: A friendly look over the fence: a neighbour with a mug of tea waving at a row of sheds labelled Ethereum, Base, Polygon, Arbitrum and Tezos]

I ran a version of the survey across Ethereum, Base, Polygon, Arbitrum and Tezos on 16 October, starting from 301 big collections. When your own roof has leaked, you check the neighbours'.

86 of the 301 point at a project's own web server, a cloud bucket or an app host. Of 201 Ethereum-style contracts, 127 have a function that lets someone change the URI (you sell the house but keep a spare key), 35 are upgradeable, and I found a recognisable freeze function in exactly one. I dug properly into the 106 that looked riskiest: 75 fragile, 14 walking dead, 14 healthy, 2 unclear, 1 lost. Eight projects' domains don't even exist any more, and for seven of those the art only survives in an indexer's copy, held by a stranger who was never asked.

Those 106 were picked because they looked risky, so it overstates things: judging a country's health by who turns up at the emergency room on a Saturday night. But the takeaway is the same everywhere: on most chains, your art is a setting someone can change, on a server someone has to keep paying for.

Why everyone went pixel art

This is a theory, not a finding, but once it landed I couldn't unsee it.

Why did so much of this space go pixel art? Nostalgia, sure. A strong visual language, a generation raised on 8-bit games, all of that. But I think there's a boring reason sitting underneath it: pixel art is tiny, and tiny is cheap to keep alive. A sprite is a few kilobytes. You can host a whole collection for next to nothing, indefinitely, and nobody ever gets the invoice.

Now look at proper high-resolution work, or a feature-length film. Blocks is 2,500 images at around 1.8 MB each, roughly 4.5 GB. Funky Donuts is about 7 GB. Put that on a monthly plan and the meter never stops running. Ask Chemical X.

The gap gets starker on chain. I twinned every one of the 400 Bitcoin Birds, pixel art at 3 to 8 KB each, for about 8.5 STX all in. Doing the same for Blocks is estimated at 5,600 to 12,100 STX. Per piece, that's around 0.02 STX for a Bird against 2 to 5 STX for a Block.

[image: Three objects drawn to scale with the cost to put each on chain: a tiny pixel bird (3 to 8 KB, about 0.02 STX), an album-style image (about 1.8 MB, 2 to 5 STX) and a huge film reel (258 MB, around 400 STX)]

So I don't think it's a coincidence that the artists who stuck around were often the ones with the smallest files. The bill shaped the art. Anything heavy cost more to keep, so the light work stayed. The people making big, rich work hit the wall first, and I suspect plenty of them just quietly stopped. Our own V2 page is a small, sad demonstration: the pieces that still load are the tiny pixel ones.

What I'm doing about it

As declared at the top, I build Xtrata, so I'm not neutral here. Take the next bit with that in mind.

Xtrata puts files directly into Stacks itself, as inscriptions. No pin, no bucket, no subscription with someone's name on it. Forever Twins is how we're using that to rescue existing collections. The first three helper contracts, for Bitcoin Pepes, LEO Cats and Miami Degens, were written by @RaphaStacks, and the versions we've launched since are built on his originals. Each collection gets a helper contract with a sealed record of every token's art: fingerprint, size, file type. Once it's sealed, nobody can change it. Then anyone can inscribe a token's twin, the helper checks the bytes against the record, and the twin goes into the helper's custody. If you hold the original, you can swap original for twin and back whenever you like, for free.

[image: How a twin works: an original token whose image is not stored on chain, a helper contract holding a sealed record of the file's hash, size and type, and a twin token holding the actual file chunks, with a tick showing the bytes match and a two-way swap between original and twin]

Where it's at, as of 26 October: seven helper contracts live, four at-risk collections on top of the three we started with: NYC Degens, Megapont Ape Club, Bitcoin Monkeys and Bitcoin Birds. 5,820 token records sealed across those four and, once the Bitcoin Pepes, LEO Cats and Miami Degens twins are counted too, more than 2,600 twins inscribed in total. Bitcoin Birds, number three on the volume list and one whose own contract now points at a web page, is fully done: all 400 Birds are on chain. Big files over 512 KB go through a browser wizard that handles the multi-step upload.

There is one collection I'd least like to lose: the Stacks Genesis collection. It's easy to undersell, especially for someone with a talent for it, so here is what it actually was.

In 2021 the network was brand new. The first NFT platform on Stacks was Boom, an open contract that anyone could mint images on, and as far as I can tell it still is. It was a playground rather than a gallery, and nobody had tried to sell serious work from it.

We made a dedicated collection of five pieces from international names, every one of them making their debut NFT. As I remember it, they were all being offered serious money to put their work on Ethereum. We asked them to come instead to a network where nobody was watching, for fractional compensation, on the promise that it would come back to them one day, which is a difficult pitch to make to anyone with an agent. Every piece was somebody's number one, and in the end that's what we called the project: This Is Number One.

[image: The five Genesis pieces on Gamma, with their artists: Channel #1 by Orbital, This Is Number One by Chemical X, So Why Try Harder by Fatboy Slim, Now Is Not Enough by Dave Stewart and Mine by Cara Delevingne]

The sale of Cara Delevingne's Mine is on chain for anyone to check. This is where commercial NFT art on Stacks began, and it deserves a better home than a grey square.

Chemical X has handed me his master files, which makes me the link between his work and somewhere permanent. The biggest, the Dave Stewart film, is 258 MB, and Orbital’s is 80 MB. The Dave Stewart film alone should cost around 400 STX to put on chain: a small price for genuine serverless permanence.

The moral of the story

Nearly every failure in this column has the same root. The token sat in a cathedral, a chain built to last, and the art sat somewhere else: a subscription, a server, a pinning account, a gateway, somebody's goodwill. The chain did its job. The somewhere-else didn't.

[image: A stone cathedral labelled the chain on a hill, with a scatter of rented sheds, a pin-board, a water tank and a van around it, each carrying a rent notice, with artworks stored inside]

So if you're creating a collection from here on, the advice is short: don't have a somewhere-else. Put the art straight onto the chain. For me that means Xtrata, which makes the file part of the chain's own history instead of an address that might stop working. It isn't free, and big files cost real STX, but it's a one-off price rather than a monthly one, which is the difference between buying the wall and renting it forever. Just ask Megapont or This Is Number One.

It doesn't matter which chain your collection started on. Wherever it began, the idea is the same: the data ends up on Stacks, Bitcoin's layer two, tied to the original token, so it can be read from there. I should be straight about where that stands. Nothing is built yet for collections on other chains. But the approach carries over. It's optional, and it's a backup: your existing NFTs stay exactly as they are, and the copy on Stacks is simply there in case the original ever goes missing. In that cross-chain survey, the Ethereum household names that work today but point at a server the project has to keep running include Meebits, Mutant Ape Yacht Club, Otherdeed, Cool Cats, Milady and VeeFriends. If you run or hold a collection on another chain and want its art kept somewhere permanent, ask, and we'll build the bridge for you.

For collections that already exist, that's what Forever Twins is for. It's the project all this research grew out of: I needed to know which collections really needed rescuing. Because the twin's data lives on the chain itself, there's no server for anyone to stop paying for, which is what went wrong in the broken collections above. It's a bridge from the old token to data that stays put, which is more than can be said for the link it replaces.

You don't need to read a contract to find out where you stand, which is lucky, because nobody should have to. So we built a free tool for exactly that: NFT Check. Paste a link, a contract address or a wallet address (the public kind, so there's nothing to connect or sign) and it tells you where the art and its description are stored, what looks good and what worries us. For now it covers Stacks collections only, so Ethereum and other chains aren't covered yet. It only looks at whether the art can still be found, not at price or safety. Each check is a dated sample, so a collection it hasn't looked at isn't confirmed fine; it just hasn't been looked at. It doesn't keep what you paste.

The short version, for anyone who skipped to the end, as I would. Existing collections: twin them, while the server is still picking up the phone. New collections: skip the rescue and start on chain. It's the difference between fitting a seatbelt and becoming the cautionary slide in someone else's presentation.

What to do on Monday

[image: What to do on Monday: a planner page with a ticked checklist beside a laptop showing a wallet address and a green tick, and a mug of coffee]

  1. Find out where your art actually lives. Not the Gamma image, the URI on the contract. It sounds technical and isn't. Open NFT Check and paste in your wallet address, or addresses if you collect across several, and it checks everything you hold in one go. Only care about one NFT? A Gamma link or a contract address works too. It's Stacks only for now, it's free, and it takes less time than making tea.

  2. On Ethereum or another chain? NFT Check can't see you yet, but the same questions apply. Look up the token URI on a block explorer and see whether it points at a web address, IPFS or the chain itself. Keep a copy of your own files. Then get in touch with @XtrataLayers on X: we build bridges on request, so tell us which collection you care about.

  3. Save the original file somewhere you control.

  4. Ask the project where it points and who's paying for it. Lots of them can still change the URI.

  5. If you're launching, work out who pays for storage in year five before you mint anything, and then ask whether the answer should be “nobody, because it's on chain”.

  6. Give it a permanent home. Start at Forever Twins and read Preserve your collection for a guide to doing it yourself. Curious how it works first? You can check our working yourself, because you shouldn't have to take my word for it.

Somewhere, Cloudinary will keep politely telling anyone who asks that chemical-x is disabled. I'd like the art to outlast the error message.
