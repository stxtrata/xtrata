The X Chess manual

This is the long version. The board carries a short one built in, so it still
says something when nothing can be read. This is inscribed separately so it can
be corrected without rebuilding a page that cannot be rebuilt.

## What X Chess is

A chess board that is itself an inscription, reading a contract on Stacks. Every
move is a transaction. Every position on screen is replayed from the chain rather
than stored anywhere, which is why the board says so much about what it is
reading: it is deriving what you see, not being handed it.

There is no server. Nothing here can be taken down, and nothing here can be
edited either, which is the same property read twice.

### What the contract stores, and what it does not

The contract stores a game's opener, the block it opened at, whether it was
offered as ranked, a 32-byte commitment to its rules, and every submission sent
to it in order. That is all.

It does NOT store the players. Their addresses live inside the rules that the
commitment hashes, and nowhere else, which is why a game with no submissions
cannot be attributed to anybody by reading the chain.

It does not store results either. A result is replayed from the submissions by
whoever is looking, and two boards that disagree about a result disagree about
their own code rather than about the chain.

## Playing a game

### Opening one

Open a game from the Play tab. You choose who may play each side: a named
address, anyone, anyone but you, or whoever moves first. Those choices are hashed
into the rules commitment, so they are fixed at the moment the game opens and
cannot be revised afterwards.

A ranked game must name both players by address. That is what makes a rating
meaningful, and the board refuses to open a ranked game any other way.

### Making a move

Click the piece, click the destination. The board refuses moves it can prove will
not count, and warns about ones it merely doubts, and the warning can always be
overruled on a second click.

A move costs a network fee and nothing the contract charges.

### Fees, and why your wallet is wrong

Wallets estimate a fee from the whole network rather than from this contract, and
have quoted between eight and fifteen times what moves here actually confirm at.
The number to use is on screen in every game.

The board cannot propose it. The wallet method it signs through takes six
parameters and a fee is not one of them, so the figure in your wallet is the
wallet's own, and yours to change.

- Xverse: press Edit, then Custom.
- Leather: choose the Custom tab.

### When a move will not confirm

Replace it at the SAME nonce with a higher fee. The board shows that nonce
whenever a move of yours is pending, because it is the one thing neither wallet
can tell you: they know your pending transactions, and not which of them is a
chess move.

Signing a new move instead takes the next nonce, which queues behind the stuck
one rather than replacing it. Two fees, and the second cannot confirm until the
first does.

- Xverse: Speed Up on the Stacks dashboard, or Edit nonce when signing.
- Leather extension: the Activity tab, then increase the fee. Leather desktop
  cannot.

### Games that never end

The contract has no clock and no resignation by absence. A game nobody has
touched for a year is, on chain, live and waiting for a move anybody may still
make.

So a board cannot tell a player who walked away from a player who is thinking,
and this one does not pretend to. It says how long a game has been quiet and
leaves the conclusion to you.

A game ends when the chain says so: checkmate, stalemate, a draw by repetition or
the fifty-move rule, a resignation, or a draw offered and accepted. There is no
other way, and in particular no organiser can declare one.

## Tournaments

### What a tournament actually is

One inscribed document naming its entrants and saying which game id is which
pairing. Nothing else. Everything the board shows about a tournament is derived
from the chain.

Nothing in it is trusted. Every pairing is checked against the rules hash its
game committed to, and a claim that does not match reads as unverified and scores
nothing. A manifest that lies fails; it does not mislead.

### Committed and compiled

A manifest inscribed BEFORE the first move of any of its games reads as
committed. One written afterwards reads as compiled.

Both are honest. Only the first is a promise: it was written when nobody knew how
the games would go. A compiled manifest is a description of games that already
existed, and anybody can write one of those about any games, including games
another manifest also claims.

### How the board finds them

By reading a wallet the organiser sends manifests to, newest first. You never
need to know a number, though you can type one at the bottom of the Tournaments
tab for a tournament this board does not watch for.

Holding finds it; creating proves it. Anybody may send an inscription to any
wallet, so a document arriving there is a claim. The board reports whether the
wallet MINTED it, which a transfer cannot fake, and marks the rest as inscribed
by somebody else.

## Running your own tournament

Most tournaments are between people, and nothing about that needs an engine, a
program or a director sitting at a keyboard. Start here; AI players are an
addition to this, not a different thing.

The tooling signs transactions, so it lives in the repository rather than in the
board. A board holds no keys and never will, being an inscription itself.

### A tournament between people

An entrant is a name and a Stacks address, and that is the whole requirement.
Each player moves from their own wallet, whenever they like, paying their own
network fee. Nobody has to be online for the tournament to progress, and there is
no server that could stop it.

Collect the addresses of everybody playing. Decide the format: a round robin
plays every pair once, a double round robin plays every pair twice so both have
White once. Six entrants is fifteen games as a single round robin, thirty as a
double.

You do not have to run a format to completion. A tournament may be any number of
its rounds, and the second exhibition was rounds eight to ten of a double round
robin, played long after the first seven.

### The order, and why it is forced

$ node harness/wizards/run-tournament.mjs open --live
$ node harness/wizards/build-manifest.mjs --name "Your Event" --format round-robin
$ node harness/wizards/inscribe-manifest.mjs --file harness/wizards/manifest-round-robin.json --live

Open the games first, because a manifest names them by id and ids do not exist
until they are opened. Build the manifest from what is actually on chain.
Inscribe it. Then the players play.

Opening does not spoil the order: a game with no submissions has no first move,
so a manifest inscribed while every game sits empty still reads as committed.

### Opening the games

The open command computes each pairing's rules hash, looks for a game already
carrying it, and opens only what is missing. It is safe to re-run, because it
matches on the hash rather than on who opened what, so it will not open a second
copy of a pairing that already exists.

Opening costs the contract's open fee per game and settles nothing else.

### Building the manifest

The builder reads every game on the contract and matches pairings BY RULES HASH
rather than by position in a list. Several games opened at once land in whatever
order the chain mines them, so a game's number says nothing about who is in it.

It refuses to write a manifest naming a game that does not exist, and refuses one
whose pairings do not match the chain. Either would be a document whose only
purpose is to be rejected.

Use --from and --rounds to take part of a format.

### Inscribing it

$ node harness/wizards/inscribe-manifest.mjs --file <path> --after <previous id> --live

The fee unit is read live rather than remembered. --after names an earlier
manifest, which lets a reader walk backwards through previous tournaments without
being told an address. It cannot be added later, so decide before inscribing.

Send the finished inscription to the wallet the board reads, or it will only be
findable by number.

### Naming the important games

A manifest may say what a game is: Final, Semi-final, Quarter-final, or anything
else a tournament calls its stages. The board shows that beside the players, in
the game itself, and in the browser tab, so a reader who opens one game knows
which it is.

Most formats do not have a final, and the board will not invent one. A round
robin plays every pair against every pair, so its last round is simply the last
round. A knockout is different: there the structure means it, and the board works
the stages out from the rounds without being told.

### Telling the players

Give them the tournament's inscription number, or point them at a board that
watches the wallet you sent it to. From there each player finds their own games:
the board reads their transaction history, so a game waiting on them is on their
screen whether or not anybody told them about it.

## Adding AI players

Everything above is unchanged. An AI player is an entrant like any other, with an
address and a wallet, and a tournament may mix them freely with people.

The difference is only who decides the moves, and it has one consequence worth
being honest about: an AI player does not move by itself. Something has to be
running that chooses for it, so a tournament of nothing but programs stops the
moment the person running them stops. The board says so when it can tell.

### Declaring which is which

Mark an entrant with a kind, so the board can warn readers rather than leaving
them to wonder why nothing has moved for a day. An entrant that says nothing is
treated as unknown rather than as a person, because the first two exhibitions
said nothing and were played entirely by programs.

### The engine every player is handed

Inscription #2991. It is deterministic, and it is not a recommendation: it ranks
every legal move with a score in centipawns from the mover's point of view, best
first, and marks any mate.

A character reads that list and picks by style. That is the only thing a
character controls, which is why two characters given identical positions play
differently, and why none of them hangs a queen.

### Why the engine exists

Measured rather than assumed. Across six on-chain games, more thinking effort
moved the blunder rate from 29% to 29%. Competence instructions moved it from 21%
to 21%. A king-mobility hint produced no mate in fifty plies where doing nothing
mated in twenty-three.

The engine halved it, 24% to 12%, replicated across three twelve-game runs. It
does not make games finish, though: a one-ply control mated more often, 8 to 6.

### Designing a character

A character sheet is an inscription describing a style: how it opens, what it
will risk, what it avoids, how it treats a won position. Keep it about
preferences rather than about chess ability, because the engine already supplies
the ability and a character that argues with it plays worse.

Inscribe the sheet, then name its id as the entry for that entrant in the
tournament manifest. The board links it beside that player, so anybody reading
the tournament can open the character and see what that competitor was told to
do.

## Rating checkpoints

Ratings are derived by replaying every ranked game in order, and that walk grows
for the life of the contract. At nine games it was free. At thirty-eight it is
half a minute. It does not stop growing.

A checkpoint is a walk somebody already did, inscribed so nobody has to do it
again from the beginning. The board reads the newest one, continues from where
it stopped, and replays everything after it exactly as before.

### It is a claim, and the board says so

Nothing before the checkpoint was replayed by the board showing it. That is
stated on the leaderboard, and Verify from scratch does the full walk and
ignores every checkpoint. A claim nobody can test is only an assertion.

So a checkpoint must name its inputs. Elo is path dependent — the same games in
another order give different ratings — and a table on its own could not be
checked against anything. Listing the games, in the order they were consumed,
makes any single claim checkable with one game's replay instead of all of them.

### Who may write one

Only xtrata.btc. Every other document this board reads is verified against the
chain or is prose, so it is enough to say who inscribed it and let the reader
judge. A checkpoint is different: the table is believed rather than checked. So
the board requires that the wallet MINTED it, and refuses one that merely arrived
there, which anybody can arrange.

That holds until a contract or a later board can do better.

### The format

$ X-CHESS-RATINGS/1
$ { "contract": "SP3JN....xchess-core-v1-canary",
$   "rankedIndex": 38, "block": 8795901,
$   "table": [{ "who": "SP...", "rating": 1290, "games": 10, "won": 7, "drawn": 3, "lost": 0 }],
$   "games": [{ "id": 13, "white": "SP...", "black": "SP...", "result": "0-1" }],
$   "note": "chain the next one to this inscription" }

rankedIndex is a position in the contract's ranked index, not a game id and not a
block: a reader continues at that position and needs no arithmetic. It must equal
the number of games listed, and the board refuses it otherwise. block is the
height the walk was made at, so a reader can see how stale it is.

### Chaining them

Every checkpoint after the first names the one before it as a dependency:

$ node harness/wizards/inscribe-manifest.mjs --file checkpoint.json --after <previous id> --live

That makes the series walkable backwards from any one of them, without being
told an address and without the wallet still holding anything. It cannot be
added afterwards. When checkpoints are no longer needed the chain simply stops;
nothing has to be withdrawn, because nothing ever can be.

## What the board refuses to do

- It will not say a game is yours unless the rules hashing to that game's own
  commitment name you.
- It will not count a tournament game whose pairing does not match the chain.
- It will not report a result it has not replayed itself.
- It will not call a tournament finished, or crown a winner, until every game has
  a result and every pairing is verified.
- It will not break a tie to award a place that was shared.
- It will not treat a rate limit as an answer: a lookup that failed is never
  recorded as a thing that does not exist.

## Glossary

commitment :: The 32-byte hash a game stores instead of its rules. The rules are
recovered by proposing candidates and testing them against it.

compiled :: A manifest inscribed after play began. A description of games that
already existed, rather than a promise made before them.

committed :: A manifest inscribed before the first move of any of its games.

directory :: A wallet that manifests are sent to, so a board can find them. The
board knows one address per group and nothing else.

entry :: The inscription defining how an AI player chooses among ranked moves.

manifest :: An inscribed document naming a tournament's entrants and which game
id is which pairing.

nonce :: The number ordering transactions from one account. Replacing a stuck
move means signing the same one again at a higher fee.

ranked :: A game that counts towards ratings. It must name both players by
address, start from the standard position, and be decided by those two alone.

replay :: Deriving the position and result from the submissions in order. The
board does this every time rather than storing an answer.

rules hash :: The commitment, used as a pairing's identity. The same two players
on the same colours produce the same hash, which is how a tournament's games are
found.

skipped :: A submission the contract stored and replay did not accept. It is on
chain permanently and it cost its sender a fee.

unverified :: A tournament pairing whose claim does not match the chain. Listed,
never counted.
