The chart that has to be right

106,000 tracks were uploaded to streaming services every day last year. 88% of everything on them was played fewer than a thousand times. What decides which records escape that is invisible, unaccountable, and in most cases purchasable. This is an attempt at something else.

A1  ·  The loop

Listen. Commit. Then find out.

You hear a song and you know nothing about it. No artist name, no artwork, no play count, no label, no follower count. You listen properly, all the way through.

Then you answer one question. Not a form, not a rating out of ten. One question: how far do you think this travels with people who have never heard it?

Then the screen opens up. Who made it, what everyone else thinks, who backed it before you did, and where you sit among them. Weeks later, you find out whether you were right.

That is the product. Everything else is machinery to make that last sentence mean something.

A2  ·  The mechanism

The one idea that makes this different from every rate‑this‑song service ever built.

The software guesses first

Before any human hears a new track, our software makes its own prediction. It is not clever software. It knows the genre, it knows what has done well recently, it knows what people with similar habits have done before. It makes a boring, sensible, slightly dull guess.

That guess is the starting line. You only score by beating it.

Agree with the software and you earn nothing, because you have told us nothing we did not already know. Back a record the software wrote off, and turn out to be right, and you earn a great deal.

We are not paying attention to people for having opinions. We are paying attention to people for having opinions our software could not have had.

It also means one thing happens on its own, with nobody having to police it. Piling on stops paying. By the time a record is obviously good, backing it is worth close to nothing. Being early is the only thing here worth anything at all.

This is not a trick we invented. The 2006 experiment measured every song twice, once with the crowd visible and once without, and treated the crowd-free score as the song's actual worth. We are doing the same job with software instead of a control group, and then paying people for the gap between the two.

A3  ·  The blindfold

You never see the crowd before you commit

This looks like a small interface decision. It is the most important rule we have.

Every chart ever made has the same disease. People see what is popular, so they listen to what is popular, so it becomes more popular, and eventually the chart is mostly measuring itself.

The experiment at the top of this page is the proof. Salganik, Dodds and Watts put 48 unknown songs in front of 14,341 people, split across eight parallel copies of the same site plus a control group who could not see download counts at all. Turning the counts on made the hits far bigger and the flops far deader, and it made which songs won substantially arbitrary. In their words:

"The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well."

Everything else was up for grabs. That middle band is where almost all music actually lives, and it is the entire distance between first and fortieth for the same record.

Show people the crowd, and the crowd stops telling you anything.

So we do not show you. You listen, you decide, you commit, and then you see everything.

A4  ·  The honesty

The part we would want to read first if somebody else were pitching this to us.

The rule that stops us cheating

If a market decides which songs get pushed, and being pushed is what makes a song do well, then the market can simply cause its own answer. That is not a forecast. That is a budget for making something happen.

This is not hypothetical and it is not old news. Last month, Kalshi ran a contract on the most streamed song in the US in June, with around $3m traded on it. On 29 June the streams for Malcolm Todd's Earrings jumped about 70% in a day and it went to number one. Spotify then removed over 500,000 streams it did not believe came from real listeners, and the track fell to fourth. Kalshi had already settled and paid out on the inflated figures. There is no suggestion that Todd or his team had anything to do with it. The suspicious activity was flagged by a Kalshi trader reading the streaming data.

That is a market betting on a number that the bettors could reach out and touch. Our answer to it is one sentence.

The people who forecast do not count.
The people who count do not forecast.

When it is time to settle whether a record actually did well, we hand it to a completely separate group of listeners. They never forecast on it. They do not follow the artist. They did not arrive through a shared link. They were picked at random after the forecasting closed, and they are not told they are the ones being measured. They just get handed a song, the way they get handed songs every day.

What that costs an attacker. To rig one record you would need to control something like a tenth of everybody on the platform. And because the group is picked at random after the fact and never announced, you would never know which tenth you needed.

There is a happy accident in this. The random, unbiased listening that lets us measure honestly is the same thing as the random, unbiased listening that gives unknown records a fair hearing. Fairness and honest measurement turn out to be one cost, paid once.

B1  ·  If you make records

Nobody hears your name

Not your label, not whether you have one, not your follower count, not your artwork, not your city, not who you know. The first thing anyone learns about your record is what it sounds like. For a lot of you that is the first genuinely blind hearing your music will ever get.

We will not sell you placement. Not now, not quietly, not later.

You do not have to trust our ethics on this. You only have to trust our self interest. The signal is the only thing we have. The moment an artist can pay to be surfaced, the signal is worth nothing and we have nothing left to sell to anybody. Pay to play would not be us being greedy. It would be us setting fire to the only asset we own.

We will tell you the truth about what happened

Not a play count. Play counts are the easiest number in music to fake and the least informative one to receive. You find out how many people stayed to the end, how many came back a week later without being prompted, and, often most useful of all, which scene actually responded. That is regularly not the scene the artist thought they were in.

Most records will not get a market

We would rather say that now than have you discover it. Forecasting attention is limited, and a service that surfaces everything has surfaced nothing. Records earn their way in. A slice is also promoted completely at random, deliberately, so nobody can reverse engineer the door.

We publish who backed you. We do not publish who did not.

There is no flop list. No bottom of the chart. No public record of a record that did not land. You see the full picture, privately, because you should have it. Nobody else does. Putting a permanent public verdict on a young artist's work is not a feature, and it cannot be taken back once it is done.

If it travels, it travels on something checkable

When a record starts moving here there is an actual answer to why it is being pushed, and the answer has names and dates in it. Nobody has to take our word for it, including you.

B2  ·  If you listen

You have been doing this free for twenty years

You found the band before your friends did. You sent the album to six people in 2019. You were right, there is no record anywhere that you were right, and the platform that monetised your instinct did not so much as thank you. Here, being early is the entire point, and it counts.

Your feed comes from people, not a black box

Not "listeners like you", which is a phrase that means nothing. Real, specific people whose calls have lined up with yours in the past, tracked and proven over time. Something more like:

These eleven people backed five records you later loved, before almost anyone else did. Here is what they are backing now.

No recommendation engine can currently say that sentence to you. It is a better sentence than anything a black box produces, because you can interrogate it.

Being good at one thing does not make you an authority on everything

You might be excellent at spotting ambient records and hopeless at pop. Most people are like this. Your standing is specific to the scenes where you have actually demonstrated it, and it does not leak into places you have not earned it.

You earn it slowly, and that is deliberate

A hot streak of three does not make you a tastemaker, it makes you lucky. Being right a few times moves almost nothing. Being right repeatedly, over time, in a scene you know, moves a lot. Closer to a chess rating than a follower count, and it cannot be bought at any price.

You will never be able to buy your way into what people hear

Reputation is the only thing that moves what gets shown to other listeners, and reputation cannot be bought at any price. It is earned from calls that resolved, and from nothing else. No amount of money, from you, from a label, or from us, moves a record in front of somebody.

There may in time be a version where people can take a real financial position, in places where that is legal. If that happens it will be a separate thing sitting alongside this one, and it will buy exposure to the outcome rather than a place in anyone's feed. Those two are kept apart deliberately and permanently. We are writing it down in public because it is the promise worth holding us to.

It does ask something of you

Sitting through a song you have never heard, all the way, not knowing if it is any good, is more work than skipping. We are not going to pretend otherwise. We think there are plenty of people who would actively enjoy that, because they already do it for nothing.

B3  ·  What changes

Three doors, two of them for sale

Today there are three ways into a listener's ears. Playlist editors, algorithms, and marketing spend. All three are opaque and two of them are purchasable.

Discovery gets an audit trail

A position on this chart is not a number that appeared. It is a list of people who said something before it was obvious, on a date, with a record of what they said. Charts today are either counting, which rewards whoever already had attention, or somebody's opinion, which rewards whoever knows the right person.

Being early becomes an asset that belongs to the person

Your record of good calls is stamped permanently and cannot be edited afterwards, including by us. If we go under, or get bought by somebody worse, it survives us. Your reputation is not our property, which is a genuinely unusual thing to be able to say.

Finding music early stops being a budget item

Right now, new music discovery at any scale requires a label's resources. If a crowd genuinely does it better, then small labels, promoters, venues, radio shows and blogs get the same instrument the majors have, at roughly no cost. That is a bigger change to the industry than anything happening on the streaming side.

Artists get a route in that is not money

Not a better route than money. Not yet. A route that money cannot buy, which is not currently on offer anywhere.

B4  ·  The small print

We would rather lose you here than later.

What we are not pretending

We are not replacing Spotify

Replacing a streaming service means winning a licensing war against three major labels, and nobody wins that starting from nothing. We want to be the thing that finds records first. If we are genuinely good at that, the streaming services and the labels become customers rather than competitors, and honestly that is the better business anyway.

Crowds are not automatically right

A room full of people can be sincerely and unanimously wrong. That is why we measure ourselves against dumb software forever, and why we will publish the comparison. If the crowd cannot beat a boring algorithm, this should not exist, and we would like to be the ones who find that out.

Whoever turns up will define the chart

Early on that will be a narrower group of people than we would like, and the results will reflect their tastes while sounding objective. Being honest about whose opinion a chart represents is the minimum. Fixing it is slow, expensive recruitment work, and no clever mechanism does it for us.

We do not yet know whether good taste is a real thing

Meaning whether it is a repeatable skill some people have, or luck that looks like skill in hindsight. That is not us being modest. It is a genuinely open question, it is testable, and it is the first thing we are testing, because if the answer is no then most of this page is fiction.

None of this is built

This is a design and an argument. Nothing above is a product you can use today.

B5  ·  First move

It starts with the cheap question

Not with markets. Not with technology. With a two week test of one thing.

Will anybody sit and listen to unknown music, properly, more than once?

A page. A queue of unreleased tracks. No scores, no credits, no predictions, no accounts beyond an email. We watch how many people finish the first song, how many songs they get through, and how many come back a week later.

If the answer is no, we will say so, and this goes in a drawer. Everything above depends on that one behaviour, and it costs almost nothing to find out. We would rather ask the cheap question first.
