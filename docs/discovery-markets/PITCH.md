# The chart that has to be right

A plain explanation of what we are trying to build, who it is for, and why we think
it changes how music gets found.

No jargon. If something here needs a diagram to understand, we have designed it
badly.

---

## The problem, from both ends

106,000 tracks a day were uploaded to streaming services last year. There are now
253 million of them sitting there, and 88% were played fewer than a thousand times.
120.5 million were played fewer than ten times.

If you are an artist without a marketing budget or a contact who runs a playlist,
that is the entire distribution story. You release a record, it lands in the same
ocean as everything else that day, and the water closes over it.

If you are a listener, you are on the other side of the same problem. Something
decides what reaches you. You do not know what it is, you cannot ask it why, and
you get no credit for the times you were the one who found something first.

Both of those problems have the same shape. The thing that decides what gets heard
is invisible, unaccountable, and in two out of three cases, purchasable.

---

## What we are building

You hear a song. You know nothing about it. No artist name, no artwork, no play
count, no label, no follower count, nothing.

You listen to it properly, all the way through.

Then you answer one question. Not a form, not a rating out of ten, one question:
how far do you think this will travel with people who have never heard it?

Then the screen opens up. You find out who made it, what everyone else thinks, who
backed it before you, and where you sit among them.

Weeks later, you find out whether you were right.

That is the whole product. Everything else is machinery to make that last sentence
mean something.

---

## The clever bit, explained without cleverness

Here is the part that makes this different from every "rate this song" service that
has ever existed.

**Before any human hears a new track, our software makes its own guess.**

It is not clever software. It knows the genre. It knows what has done well
recently. It knows what people with similar habits have done before. It makes a
boring, sensible, slightly dull prediction.

That guess is the starting line.

**You only score by beating it.**

If you agree with the software, you earn nothing, because you have told us nothing
we did not already know. If you back a record the software wrote off, and you turn
out to be right, you earn a great deal.

That is really the whole idea. We are not paying attention to people for having
opinions. We are paying attention to people for having opinions our software could
not have had.

It also means one thing automatically, without us having to police it: **piling on
stops paying.** By the time a record is obviously good, backing it is worth almost
nothing. Being early is the only thing here that is worth anything at all.

## Why you cannot see what anyone else thinks

You commit before you see the crowd. Always.

This looks like a small interface decision. It is the most important rule we have.

Every chart ever made has the same disease. People see what is popular, so they
listen to what is popular, so it becomes more popular, and eventually the chart is
mostly measuring itself.

There is a published experiment on exactly this. In 2006 Salganik, Dodds and Watts
put 48 songs by unknown bands in front of 14,341 people, split across eight
identical copies of the same site running at the same time, plus a control group
who could not see download counts at all. Turning the counts on made the hits far
bigger, the flops far deader, and which songs won substantially arbitrary. In their
words, "the best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well", and
everything in between was up for grabs. That middle band is where almost all music
actually lives.

One record makes the point on its own. Lockdown, by a Milwaukee band called
52metro, finished first out of 48 in one world and fortieth in another. Same song,
same competition, indistinguishable audiences. The entire difference was whether
you could see what the people before you had downloaded.

Show people the crowd, and the crowd stops telling you anything.

So we do not show you. You listen, you decide, you commit, and then you see
everything.

## The rule that stops us cheating

This is the part we would want to read first if someone else were pitching this to
us.

If a market decides which songs get pushed, and being pushed is what makes a song
do well, then the market can simply cause its own answer. That is not a forecast.
That is a budget for making something happen.

This is not hypothetical and it is not old news. Last month Kalshi ran a contract
on the most streamed song in the US in June, with around $3m traded on it. On 29
June the streams for Malcolm Todd's Earrings jumped about 70% in a day and it went
to number one. Spotify then removed over 500,000 streams it did not believe came
from real listeners, and the track fell to fourth. Kalshi had already settled and
paid out on the inflated figures. There is no suggestion that Todd or his team had
anything to do with it. A Kalshi trader reading the streaming data is who flagged
it.

Our answer is one sentence:

> **The people who forecast do not count. The people who count do not forecast.**

When it is time to settle whether a record actually did well, we hand it to a
completely separate group of listeners. They never forecast on it. They do not
follow the artist. They did not arrive through a shared link. They were picked at
random after all the forecasting closed, and they are not told they are the ones
being measured.

They just get handed a song, at random, the way they get handed songs every day.

**What that costs an attacker.** To rig one record, you would need to control
something like a tenth of everybody on the platform. And because the group is
picked at random after the fact and never announced, you would never know which
tenth you needed. That is the difference between a system that can be gamed by a
determined person with a laptop and one that cannot be gamed without owning the
place.

There is a happy accident in this. The random, unbiased listening that lets us
measure honestly is the exact same thing as the random, unbiased listening that
gives unknown records a fair hearing. Fairness and honest measurement turn out to
be the same cost, paid once.

## For artists

**Nobody hears your name.**

Not your label, not whether you have one, not your follower count, not your
artwork, not your city, not who you know. The first thing anyone learns about your
record is what it sounds like. For a lot of you that is the first genuinely blind
hearing your music will ever get.

**We will not sell you placement. Not now, not later, not quietly.**

You do not have to trust our ethics on this. You only have to trust our
self interest. The signal is the only thing we have. The moment an artist can pay
to be surfaced, the signal is worth nothing, and we have nothing left to sell to
anybody. Pay to play would not be us being greedy. It would be us setting fire to
the only asset we own.

**We will tell you the truth about what happened.**

Not a play count. Play counts are the easiest number in music to fake and the least
informative one to receive. You will find out how many people stayed to the end,
how many came back a week later without being prompted, and, often the most useful
of all, which scene actually responded. That is regularly not the scene the artist
thought they were in, and knowing it is worth more than a thousand streams.

**Most records will not get a market.**

We would rather say that now than have you discover it. Forecasting attention is
limited, and a service that surfaces everything has surfaced nothing. Records earn
their way in. A slice is also promoted completely at random, deliberately, so that
nobody can reverse engineer the door.

**We publish who backed you. We do not publish who did not.**

There is no flop list. No bottom of the chart. No public record of a record that
did not land. You will see the full picture, privately, because you should have it.
Nobody else will. Putting a permanent public verdict on a young artist's work is
not a feature, and it cannot be taken back once it is done.

**If it does travel, it travels on something that can be checked.**

When a record starts moving here, there is an actual answer to "why is this being
pushed", and the answer has names and dates in it. Nobody involved has to take our
word for it, including you.

## For listeners

**You have been doing this for free for twenty years.**

You found the band before your friends did. You sent the album to six people in
2019. You were right, and there is no record anywhere that you were right, and the
platform that monetised your instinct did not so much as thank you.

Here, being early is the entire point, and it counts.

**Your feed comes from people, not from a black box.**

Not "listeners like you", which is a phrase that means nothing. Real, specific
people whose calls have actually lined up with yours in the past, tracked and
proven over time. Something more like:

> These eleven people backed five records you later loved, before almost anyone
> else did. Here is what they are backing now.

No recommendation engine can currently say that sentence to you. It is a better
sentence than anything a black box produces, because you can interrogate it.

**Being good at one thing does not make you an authority on everything.**

You might be excellent at spotting ambient records and hopeless at pop. Most people
are like this. Your standing here is specific to the scenes where you have actually
demonstrated it, and it does not leak into places you have not earned it.

**You have to earn it slowly, and that is deliberate.**

A hot streak of three does not make you a tastemaker, it makes you lucky. Being
right a few times moves almost nothing. Being right repeatedly, over time, in a
scene you know, moves a lot. It is closer to a chess rating than a follower count,
and it cannot be bought at any price.

**This is not gambling, and it structurally cannot become gambling.**

You cannot put money in. You cannot take money out. Credits are free, they arrive
on a schedule, you cannot buy more, and you cannot give them to anyone else.

If this service ever adds a "buy more credits" button, it will have become a
different and much worse company, and you should leave immediately. We are writing
that down in public on purpose.

**It does ask something of you.**

Sitting through a song you have never heard, all the way, not knowing if it is any
good, is more work than skipping. We are not going to pretend otherwise. We think
there are plenty of people who would actively enjoy that, because they already do
it for nothing. If we are wrong about that, nothing else here matters, and we would
rather find out in the first fortnight than the third year.

## What this changes about how music gets distributed

Today there are three doors into a listener's ears. Playlist editors, algorithms,
and marketing spend. All three are opaque, and two of them are purchasable.

**Discovery gets an audit trail.** A position on this chart is not a number that
appeared. It is a list of people who said something before it was obvious, on a
date, with a record of what they said. Charts today are either counting, which
rewards whoever already had attention, or somebody's opinion, which rewards whoever
knows the right person. This is an attempt at a third thing.

**Being early becomes an asset that belongs to the person.** Your record of good
calls is stamped permanently and cannot be edited afterwards, including by us. If
we go under, or get bought by somebody worse, it survives us. Your reputation is
not our property, which is a genuinely unusual thing to be able to say.

**Finding music early stops being a budget item.** Right now, doing new music
discovery at any scale requires a label's resources. If a crowd genuinely does it
better, then small labels, promoters, venues, radio shows and blogs get access to
the same instrument the majors have, at roughly no cost. That is a much bigger
change to the industry than anything that happens on the streaming side.

**Artists get a route in that is not money.** Not a better route than money, not
yet. A route that money cannot buy, which is not currently on offer anywhere.

## What we are not pretending

We would rather lose you here than later.

**We are not replacing Spotify.** Replacing a streaming service means winning a
licensing war against three major labels, and nobody wins that starting from
nothing. We want to be the thing that finds records first. If we are genuinely good
at that, the streaming services and the labels become customers rather than
competitors, and honestly that is the better business anyway.

**Crowds are not automatically right.** A room full of people can be sincerely and
unanimously wrong. That is why we measure ourselves against dumb software forever,
and why we will publish the comparison. If the crowd cannot beat a boring algorithm,
this should not exist, and we would like to be the ones who find that out.

**Whoever turns up will define the chart.** Early on that will be a narrower group
of people than we would like, and the results will reflect their tastes while
sounding objective. Being honest about whose opinion a chart represents is the
minimum. Fixing it is slow, expensive recruitment work, and there is no clever
mechanism that does it for us.

**We do not yet know whether good taste is a real thing.** Meaning whether it is a
repeatable skill some people have, or luck that looks like skill in hindsight. That
is not us being modest. It is a genuinely open question, it is testable, and it is
the first thing we are testing, because if the answer is no then most of this
document is fiction.

**None of this is built.** This is a design and an argument. Nothing above is a
product you can use today.

---

## Where it starts

Not with markets. Not with technology. With a two week test of one question:

**Will anybody sit and listen to unknown music, properly, more than once?**

A page. A queue of unreleased tracks. No scores, no credits, no predictions, no
accounts beyond an email. We watch how many people finish the first song, how many
songs they get through, and how many come back a week later.

If the answer is no, we will say so, and this idea goes in a drawer. Everything
above depends on that one behaviour, and it costs almost nothing to find out.

We would rather ask the cheap question first.

---

Every chart you have ever seen was made either by counting or by somebody's
opinion. Counting rewards whoever already had the attention. Opinion rewards
whoever knows the right person.

We would like to try a chart made of people who have to be right.
